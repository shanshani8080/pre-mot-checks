Pre-MOT Checks You Can Do on Your Own Driveway

A practical walk-round to do the weekend before an MOT: lights, tyres, wipers, brakes, seatbelts, warning lights and the small faults that cause most fails.

Updated 25 November 2026 · 4 min read

A large share of MOT failures are caused by faults an owner could have found in half an hour with a torch and a helper. Testers are not trying to catch anyone out, but they have to fail a car that does not meet the standard on the day. Looking back at an mot history check for your own car is a sensible first step, because last year's advisories are often this year's fails. After that, work through the checks below on a dry afternoon.

Lights and indicators

Lighting faults are the most common single reason cars fail. Ask someone to stand outside while you work through every switch.

  • Dipped and main beam headlights, both sides.
  • Front and rear sidelights.
  • All four indicators plus any side repeaters, and the hazard lights.
  • Brake lights, including the high level one in the rear screen or spoiler.
  • Rear fog light and the warning lamp that goes with it on the dashboard.
  • Number plate lamps, which are easy to forget and often fail.

Look at the lenses too. A cracked lens that lets in water or shows white light at the rear can be marked as a defect.

Tyres and wheels

The legal minimum tread depth for a car is 1.6mm across the central three quarters of the tyre, all the way round. A 20p coin pushed into the main grooves is a rough guide: if you can see the outer band of the coin, the tread is getting close. Turn the steering to full lock so you can see the inner edge of the front tyres, which often wears faster than the outside.

  • Check for cuts, bulges or exposed cord in the sidewalls.
  • Make sure tyres on the same axle are the same size and type.
  • Look for missing wheel nuts or damaged rims.

Windscreen, wipers and washers

Damage larger than 10mm in the area directly in front of the driver, or larger than 40mm elsewhere in the area the wipers clear, will fail. Small chips can usually be repaired cheaply if you get to them early. Wiper blades should clear the screen without leaving smears, and the washers need to spray onto the glass. Fill the washer bottle before you go, because an empty bottle is a fail.

Inside the cabin

  • Pull each seatbelt sharply to check it locks, then let it retract fully.
  • Look for fraying or cuts in the belt webbing.
  • Press the horn. It should give one continuous note.
  • Check that the driver's seat is secure and adjusts properly.
  • Make sure the doors open from inside and outside.

Warning lights on the dashboard

Turn the ignition on and watch the dashboard. The warning lights should come on and then go out once the engine starts. An engine management, ABS, airbag, power steering or brake fluid warning light that stays on can lead to a fail, so it is worth having the fault read before the test rather than after.

Brakes and fluids

You cannot test brake performance at home in the way a roller tester can, but you can listen for grinding, feel for pulling to one side and check the handbrake holds the car on a slope. Under the bonnet, check that the brake fluid sits between the minimum and maximum marks. Check the oil level as well, since testers may decline to carry out the emissions part of the test on an engine that is very low on oil.

Final tidy up

Clear the boot and the footwells. A tester can refuse to test a car that is too dirty or cluttered to inspect properly. Clean the number plates, check they are not cracked or faded, and make sure the fuel cap seals.
